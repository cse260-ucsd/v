Warp tiling

Warp tiling gives each warp of a block its own 4 × 4 square of C, where Part 4's warps each took a 2 × 8 strip. Each thread keeps one sum in each 2 × 2 sub-tile of its warp's square, so the warp's 4 threads fill one sub-tile together, from 2 neighboring numbers of As and 2 of Bs. Threads read and compute as much as in Part 4, but at every step a warp needs 4 numbers of As and 4 of Bs, where Part 4's needed 2 and 8.

In a real implementation

  1. Tile sizes. The warp tile, its sub-tiles and each thread's piece of a sub-tile, 1 number here, are all parameters to tune. A WM × WN warp tile needs WM + WN numbers of As and Bs per step of k for WM · WN multiply-adds, which is why CUTLASS recommends a large warp tile. A Tesla T4 warp has 32 threads, so a 32 × 64 warp tile gives each thread 64 sums, for instance a 4 × 4 piece of each of 4 sub-tiles. Time a few combinations on the T4 to find the fastest.
  2. Banks. In Part 4, a warp of 32 threads along a row of the block reads Bs two numbers apart, two threads to a bank. Here every read of a warp asks for a few neighbors. With 32 threads as 4 × 8 in each sub-tile and one number each, a read of b takes 8 neighbors from a row of Bs, each in its own bank and broadcast to 4 threads. A read of a takes 4 neighbors from a column of As, which lie a row of As, BK numbers, apart, so they share banks once BK reaches 16. Storing As transposed, with k along its rows, puts them side by side in memory too.
  3. Tensor Cores. Every multiply-add here runs on the T4's 2,560 FP32 cores, 8.1 TFLOPS at most. CUTLASS on a T4 mostly uses its 320 Tensor Cores instead: all 32 threads of a warp call one warp matrix function together, which multiplies, for instance, a 16 × 16 tile of half-precision A by a 16 × 16 tile of B, for up to 65 TFLOPS. A warp tile is then a grid of these multiplies: the innermost loops of CUTLASS's loop nest.
  4. Libraries. Programs rarely write this kernel themselves. They call cuBLAS (cublasSgemm) or build a kernel from CUTLASS's templates.
